Every tutorial so far has folded at most once. This one folds twice, and shows
what happens to the paper in between: it stacks. Once there is a stack, a new
fold has to decide how far down it reaches — and `up to` is how you control
that.

## One fold, two layers

Start with a single fold.

<Beloch filename="one-fold.bel">
paper square
fold map .d onto .a
</Beloch>

`fold map .d onto .a` folds `.d` (top-left) onto `.a` (bottom-left) — the same
kind of reflection from earlier tutorials, but this time it's a `fold`, not a
`mark`, so the paper actually moves. Toggle to the folded view: the top half of
the sheet is now lying on top of the bottom half. The paper is no longer one
flat face — it is two stacked layers.

## A second fold goes through the whole stack

Fold again, along a new crease.

<Beloch filename="two-folds.bel">
paper square
fold map .d onto .a
fold map .c onto .d
</Beloch>

`fold map .c onto .d` creases a new line and folds along it. By default, a
fold goes through *every* layer beneath its crease — top and bottom alike. In
the folded view you can see the result: 4 faces. Both layers from the first
fold got creased by the second.

It helps to see *where* that second crease falls before the paper moves. Here
is the same second crease — named `--second` so we can point at it — ghosted
onto the one-fold stack:

<Beloch filename="two-folds.bel" isometry={1} textureUpTo={2}>
paper square
fold map .d onto .a
fold --second = map .c onto .d
</Beloch>

The dotted line is `--second` projected onto the stack from the first fold. It
runs straight down the middle, crossing *both* layers at once — which is why
folding along it creases all of them into the 4 faces above. (This is a static
"ghost" view: the geometry is the one-fold stack, the decoration is a crease
from a later step.)

## `up to` limits the fold to one flap

That second fold cut straight across the whole stack, so it swept up both
layers. To fold *just* the top layer, the new crease has to stay parallel to
the one already separating the layers — otherwise lifting it would drag the
bottom layer along too. Here's one that works: fold the top down as before,
find the point where that hinge crosses the left edge, and fold `.d` back up
onto it.

<Beloch filename="up-to.bel">
paper square
mark --left = through .a .d
fold --hinge = map .d onto .a
.h = --left * --hinge
fold map .d onto .h up to .d
</Beloch>

`--left * --hinge` is a **meet**: the point where two lines cross. `.h` sits
exactly on the first fold's crease, so folding `.d` onto it makes a new crease
parallel to that hinge — one that only cuts through the top layer to begin
with. `up to .d` then scopes the fold to the flap `.d` is on, leaving the rest
of the top layer, and the whole bottom layer, alone. Toggle to the folded
view and count faces: 3, not 4.

`up to <point>` is how you fold "just this flap" instead of dragging the whole
stack along with it — essential once a model has more than one or two layers.
(Not every second fold can be scoped this way: the flap you're lifting has to
be free to move on its own, without a hinge to the rest of the paper getting
in the way.)

## What you learned

- Folding stacks the paper: after one fold you have layers, not a single flat
  face.
- By default, a fold passes through every layer under its crease — a second
  fold on a two-layer stack creases both layers.
- `up to <point>` restricts a fold to the flap(s) that reach that point,
  leaving the other layers uncreased.

Try adjusting the anchor in the [Playground](/playground/) to see which flap
folds and which stays put.
